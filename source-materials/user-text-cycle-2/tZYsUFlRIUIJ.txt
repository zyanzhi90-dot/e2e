Passivity Preservation for Variable Impedance
Control of Compliant Robots
Emmanouil Spyrakos-Papastavridis , Peter R. N. Childs , and Jian S. Dai , Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—The notable performance exhibited by
impedance controllers during robotic interaction has
led to the widespread use of this control methodology.
Improved position and interaction control might be
attainable through utilisation of variable impedance control
(VIC) techniques. Interactional performance could be
further improved by combining structural compliance
with VIC. However, utilization of VIC tends to induce
energy-injecting elements, which could impact on a robotic
system’s stability/passivity. Additionally, implementation of
active VIC techniques on passively compliant robots has
not been investigated (although several works consider
VIC using variable stiffness actuators), which renders
the existing rigid-joint robot, passivity-inducing control
schemes inapplicable to compliant systems. To this end,
the work presented here introduces a novel scheme, termed
passivity-preservation control (PPC), which suppresses the
energy injections that could be introduced into compliant
robots, as a result of VIC. Compared to tank-based VIC
approaches the PPC scheme is directly applicable to
ﬂexible-joint robots, even ones with nonlinear passive
stiffness elements, while its performance is independent
of the tank-energy levels. Moreover, the proposed scheme
permits stable VIC using full-state feedback, thereby
enabling impedance modulations relating to both motor
and link-side variables. Consequently, full-state feedback
gains can be generated via linear quadratic regulator
optimisation, thus enabling application of gain-scheduling
techniques on ﬂexible-joint robots for enhanced position
control. Passivity and stability analyses are performed for
joint and Cartesian-space versions of the PPC scheme,
which justify their applicability to both interaction and
“free-motion” scenarios. The PPC scheme’s efﬁcacy,
compared to constant gain impedance methods, in
terms of convergence and interactional performance,

I. I NTRODUCTION
T
HE increasing necessity for operational safety, as regards
the interaction of robotic devices with their immediate en-
vironments, has stimulated collective research effort toward the
resolution of the robotic interaction problem. Traditional robotic
manipulators possessing “stiff” mechanical structures, typically
operated in position-control mode, are deemed unsuitable for
interaction tasks. Moreover, direct force control methods may
exhibit insufﬁcient levels of robustness when limited knowledge
of the desired contact forces is available, or when environmental
parameters are continuously ﬂuctuating. Thus, the majority of
environmental interaction control methods relies upon variants
of impedance control, i.e., compliance control [5], stiffness
control [6], and admittance control [6]. The impedance control
concept was introduced to the ﬁeld via [7], as a means of
allowing robots to establish user-deﬁned dynamical relation-
ships between their motions and the associated external forces
[8]. Robotics literature contains an array of works involving
impedance controllers, including some of the most fundamental
research outputs [7], [8], as well as additional examples of
robustly performing impedance controllers [9], [10].
Supplementary interaction performance enhancements can
be acquired by means of utilizing variable impedance control
(VIC) instead of invariant-impedance control (IIC), as the for-
mer bears a closer resemblance to the operation of biological
machines. Those biological machines display superior environ-
mental adaptability in comparison to their artiﬁcial counterparts,
as a consequence of their inherent impedance modulation mech-
anisms, hypothesised by [4]. The same work [4] proposes a
reinforcement learning algorithm to select appropriate values for
an impedance-gain scheduler. Ikeura and Inooka [11] describe a
VIC method relying upon translation of human muscle activity
to an array of active impedance gains for a robot controller.
Tsumugiwa et al . [12] report on a technique that facilitates
human–robot interaction between a manipulator and human
operator, via rapid impedance modulations. Blaya and Herr
[13] describe a biologically inspired study involving real-time

VIC of an orthotic joint. For the purpose of permitting smooth
adaptation to external impacts during human–robot interaction
tasks, Y anget al. [14] propose a learning controller that gradually
adjusts the robot’s total impedance and feedforward force sig-

a task-parameterized model, which enables the generation of
optimal active stiffness and damping gains using linear quadratic
regulator (LQR) optimization, in accordance with the poses
of certain environmental artefacts. Similarly, Rozo et al . [16]
extend the latter work by encoding additional state information,
while devising an optimal control method to generate Cartesian
impedance gain matrices. Gribovskaya et al . [17] describe an
algorithm that combines adaptive control and programming
by demonstration to regulate a robot’s impedance parameters.
Moreover, Spyrakos-Papastavridis et al . [18] and [19] demon-
strate that a humanoid robot can switch between heightened
tracking accuracy and compliant interaction, using VIC, for the
purpose of improving bipedal balancing performance. Ficuciello
et al . [20] present a framework for the merging of VIC with
redundancy resolution methodologies.
It is worth noting, however, that VIC is also achievable “me-
chanically,” by means of utilizing variable stiffness actuators
(VSAs) [21], [22], as has been demonstrated on DLR’s David
robot [23], [24]. Braun et al . [25] describe an algorithm that
is capable of simultaneously designing motion and impedance
trajectories for systems actuated by VSAs, thereby exhibiting
improved performance over their ﬁxed-gain counterparts. Com-
pared to other compliant robot designs, VSAs display improved
impact robustness and energy efﬁciency, extended dynamic
capability, and enhanced task adaptability [24], albeit at the
expense of higher dimensional requirements, in addition to
increased design and control complexity.
A different mechanical means of augmenting a robot’s in-
teractional performance is the incorporation of passive elastic
elements into its physical structure [26], [27], [28]. An estab-
lished, compliant-joint robot design approach entails the usage
of series elastic actuators (SEAs) [29], [30], whose inherent
ﬂexibility renders the pertinent robotic systems compliant during
interaction with their environments, thereby reducing the forces
arising during the post-impact phase. However, this beneﬁcial in-
teraction feature introduces under actuation, reduces the plant’s
natural frequency, and induces oscillatory dynamics, thereby
amplifying the complexity of the position regulation/tracking
problem [31]. Moreover, SEA-based robots are incapable of reg-
ulating their passive compliance in real-time, although their me-
chanical designs are typically more compact than those of VSAs,
as they comprise less actuators. An established strategy entails
the implementation of impedance controllers onto SEA-driven
robots to harness their respective interactional attributes [31].
Although initial works introduced stabilizing control schemes
for ﬂexible-joint robots [32], [33], those methods were subse-
quently augmented [31] [34] via incorporation of motor iner-
tia scaling terms, torque and torque derivative feedback [34],
[35], and Cartesian impedance gains, in addition to passivity
analyses.
Since impedance controllers are typically used for interac-
tion tasks, it is frequently deemed appropriate to assess their
interaction capabilities by means of passivity analyses [35], [36],
which rely upon external-wrench-based conditions.
Implementation of active VIC schemes on SEA-powered
robots is reported in [18] and [19], from which this work
distinguishes itself, as it proposes a novel analytical controller
for stable VIC of compliant robots. Furthermore, the passivity-
preservation control (PPC) scheme uses full-state feedback [35],
[37], thereby enhancing robustness and permitting systematic
gain generation.
In spite of the heightened interaction capabilities offered by
VIC, this technique may endanger a system’s stability/passivity,
as it tends to induce energy-injecting terms [1]. To this end,
Ferraguti et al. [1] propose a tank-based approach to suppress the
relevant energy injections and, thus, preserve stability/passivity.
Ferraguti et al . [2] offer an extension to [1] by considering a
time-varying admittance controller, whose passivity is analyzed
using port-Hamiltonian representations. In contrast, Kronan-
der and Billard [3] resolves the VIC-related energy-injection
problem through a modiﬁed Lyapunov function, which imposes
restrictions on the active impedance ﬂuctuations.
The work described herein assumes the pre-existence of a con-
tinuous variable impedance trajectory (or strategy) and proposes
a novel control scheme to guarantee its stable replication by a
compliant-joint robot. Thus, the proposed scheme enables im-
plementation of gain-scheduling methods [38] on ﬂexible-joint
robots, a topic that is brieﬂy explored in this work, by means
of linearizing a compliant manipulator’s dynamics around two
points and using LQR techniques to generate a set of gains that
the system can transition between. A similar gain-tuning ap-
proach is described in [39]. The proposed method’s contributions
are synopsised as follows:
1) A stable VIC scheme for ﬂexible-joint robots, considering
time-varying active and passive stiffnesses.
2) Utilization of link position and velocity feedback gains.
The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II
provides the compliant joint model and problem statement, while
Section III proposes the novel PPC scheme in conjunction with
stability/passivity analyses. Section IV describes the impedance
modulation and gain tuning techniques, Section V presents
simulations and experimental results acquired during Sawyer’s
execution of various interaction tasks. Finally, Section VI con-
cludes this article.

C. Augmented-State Controllers
The vast majority of state-of-the-art PD-plus-type controllers
for ﬂexible-joint robots relies upon the exploitation of indirect
forms of link position/velocity feedback [31], [34], [35], coupled
with inner torque loops. This feature augments the controllers’
dimension and enhances robustness, by accounting for the link
(in addition to the motor) states, which are directly interact-
ing with the environment. Even though [32] accounts solely
for motor-side feedback, the robot considered in the relevant
simulations [32] possesses passive stiffness values exceeding
those of some contemporary SEA-powered robots [24], [27] by
more than an order of magnitude.

for external force mitigation during interaction [5], while high

values of the external contact wrenches, as is the paradigm for

capable of adapting its behavior in accordance with the envi-
ronmental conditions (contacts), thereby rendering it compliant
and safer during interaction—a feature that is precluded by IIC.

B. LQR-Based Impedance Gain Generation
Aside from their straightforward physical intuition and con-
sideration of interaction port states, one of the main beneﬁts of
control laws (12) and (22) is that they facilitate the challenging
task of full-state feedback gain generation, which would other-
wise involve the manual tuning of four distinct gain matrices.
Although sophisticated model-based, ﬂexible-joint robot gain-
tuning methods are available [42], they lead to the production
of gain matrices tailored to control designs involving torque
and torque derivative feedback [42]. Contrarily, LQR can be
used for direct generation of diagonal PPC gains, given that nu-
merous systematic LQR gain matrix diagonalization techniques

Despite the signiﬁcant differences between PPC and classical
PD-plus control, identical VIC strategies can be used with
both methods in principal, although this depends on the noise
levels, gain selection, and passive stiffness values. However, the
stability analysis indicates that ˙q

magnitudes. For practical implementation, one should account
for feedback noise, which may induce large input torque spikes
at low velocities, owing to ˙Θ

auxiliary elements are modiﬁable in real-time, the user is granted
full control over the additive control signal. Moreover, PPC
guarantees passivity during VIC, in contrast to classic PD-plus
control.

VI. C ONCLUSION
The work described here introduces a novel control method-
ology, namely PPC, which is designed to suppress the adverse
effects arising during VIC. The PPC technique enables the
implementation of stable active VIC (gain-scheduling) methods
on under-actuated, ﬂexible-joint robots, with both linear and
nonlinear passive stiffness elements. Since the PPC controller
employs full-state feedback, one can utilize well-established

Joint and Cartesian space versions of the PPC controller are

Experiments involving VCIC and IIC, using both PPC and
non-PPC schemes, enabled a comparative analysis involving
the distinct methodologies. Experimental and simulation data
reveals that employing VIC leads to the reduction of contact
force magnitudes, as compared to using IIC.
