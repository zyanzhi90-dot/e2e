A B S T R A C T 

We solve numerically the problem of a sliding viscoelastic cylinder in the presence of adhesion, modelled using the Lennard Jones force-separation law. For the material, a linear standard viscoelastic model is considered. We show the entire problem, and the friction coefficient in particular, depends on four dimensionless quantities: the Maugis–Tabor parameter 𝜆 , the dimensionless load 𝑊 , the dimensionless speed 𝑉 , and the ratio between the relaxed and the instantaneous modulus of the material, 𝑘 = 𝐸 0∕𝐸 ∞. For small 𝜆 , which corresponds to small rather rigid cylinders, friction returns to the classical Hunter solution for large compressive loads. For intermediate and large 𝜆 adhesion plays a significant role, and the ‘‘JKR limit’’ shows an adhesive component of the friction force which reaches the limit of the surface adhesion times the ratio of instantaneous to relaxed moduli at some intermediate speeds. The results obtained are in qualitative agreement with reported experimental results and can serve to guide the comparison with experiments, where it is known that the role of adhesion in rubber friction is still largely unclear and treated very often with semi-empirical equations. 

1. Introduction 

Understanding and quantifying the dissipative mechanisms happen-ing at the interface between two bodies in relative motion is one of the major scope of tribology. In particular, despite nearly a century of basic laboratory experiments and theories, understanding rubber friction is far from complete. Early basic tests showed that the friction coefficient increases (at least initially) with sliding speed and certainly with surface smoothness, but decreased with load (Schallamach, 1952). Schallamach (1952) suggested simply that the frictional force would be proportional to the Hertz contact area. Later, Schallamach (1953) showed a temperature dependence and a model for which molecular bonds detach from and attach to the substrate resulting in small jumps in the sliding direction, resulting in a shear stress whose dependence however is hard to make explicitly dependent on the parameters in the problem. Furthermore, in certain sliding conditions, Schallamach (1971) discovered a new phenomenon named ‘‘Schallamach waves’’ using soft rubber spheres on a hard smooth track, or vice versa, resulting in very high friction and which mechanism is also only partly understood even today making frictional stress rather insensitive to sliding speed, load and viscoelastic properties of rubber (Roberts and Jackson, 1975). On the other hand, the seminal experiments by Grosch (1963) demonstrated a close parallel between friction and the vis-coelastic properties of rubber, and one notable result was the collapse of many experimental data for friction into a single master curve by

The shape of the curve friction coefficient versus sliding velocity was dependent on the condition of the surface (smooth wavy glass vs abrasive paper). For the smooth wavy glass, the friction curve was nearly symmetrical and had a single peak mostly attributed to adhesion, while for abrasive paper (a rough substrate) the friction curve was not symmetric. Grosch suggested in this case that two mechanisms were at play: adhesion which resulted in a peak at lower speed, and viscoelastic energy losses arising from the deformation due to surface asperities, which resulted in a (generally higher) peak at higher speeds. In all cases the friction coefficient was found with a peak of about 2–3. Despite it is well understood that the Maugis parameter plays a crucial role in determining the interactions between adhesion and viscoelasticity, at the best of the authors knowledge, there is no study which has attempted to clarify the role of 𝜆 in the case of sliding contacts. Moreover, being 𝜆 a scale dependent parameter, assessing its influence on the friction coefficient would be of primary impor-tance to help in clarifying how frictional resistance is generated in multiscale rough sliding contacts with implications in several every-day applications. It is the purpose of the present paper to study in detail how the interplay of the mechanisms (i) and (ii) generate the frictional resis-tance, in particular with reference to the dependence of the friction coefficient on the Maugis parameter 𝜆 , the normal load 𝑊 , the sliding velocity 𝑣 , and the ratio 𝑘 = 𝐸 0∕𝐸 ∞. A general qualitative agreement with some of the experimental findings is reproduced, nevertheless we shall not claim that models like ours can predict rubber friction in general as other mechanisms that contribute to friction may be at play (Schallamach, 1952, 1971) which we have neglected here, as discussed in the Discussion and Conclusion paragraphs.

Experimental findings are not easy to explain: Grosch (1963) for ex-ample found two peaks for friction, one attributed to adhesion and the other to viscoelastic losses, but this was for the silicon carbide rough surface, not for the smooth surface. Indeed, for a smooth interface (Grosch, 1963) measured a quite symmetrical bell-shape curve for the friction coefficient, showing a single peak, which is in qualitative agreement with the results we have presented here. For a sliding smooth sinusoid (Carbone et al., 2022) reported two peaks of friction and suggested this could explain the experiments of Grosch with the rough surface. However, in our numerical experiments, we never found two peaks for the friction curve in our model which is rather that of sliding against a smooth surface. It would be useful to understand in the Grosch experiments why the adhesion peak emerged for the rough surface. Our model predicts that, at asperity level, the Maugis–Tabor parameter being probably very low, there should be no little effect of adhesion, while for the wavy smooth surface, where the Maugis–Tabor parameter is probably much larger, adhesion is generally so high that we see only its corresponding peak, since dissipation due to viscoelastic losses is so small for smooth surfaces, with respect to the adhesive component. On rough surfaces the contribution of adhesion becomes of the same order of the viscoelastic losses, but our model does not contain enough ingredients to model a rough contact. It is interesting to compare our results with those of Carbone et al. (2022), and Mandriota et al. (2024). These authors make a useful separation of symmetric and antisymmetric terms in the Green’s function, and recognize symmetric terms correspond to elastic energy and antisymmetric terms give rise to dissipation which can increase the effective work of adhesion at the trailing edge, and decrease it at the leading edge. The theory then solves the contact problem with a numerical approach using an entirely energetic model recalling the JKR limit which in standard elastic adhesive problems is applied for large values of the Maugis–Tabor parameter 𝜆 , so that we should be tempted to think it is the limit of the model we have presented here which applies for any value of 𝜆 . However, a viscoelastic adhesive contact problem is essentially a viscoelastic crack problem, for which the seminal work of Graham (1969) suggested that any energy balance approach should be supplemented by at least some details of coupling, over a finite size scale, with the microscale processes of separation (Rice, 1978). In other words, there needs to be a finite length scale in the problem associated to a cutoff stress 𝜎 𝑐 of adhesive forces like in the known (Persson and Brener, 2005) theory for a semi-infinite crack or a representative cohesive stress like in the model of Schapery (1975). Indeed, as we have discussed in a recent parallel paper (Nazari et al., 2024), our numerical solution has been fully validated with the Persson and Brener (2005) theory when the speed of sliding is low, and it can be shown that the friction force due to adhesion grows with the Maugis– Tabor parameter (see Eq. 5. Conclusions 

We have shown rather comprehensive results for the problem of a sliding viscoelastic cylinder in the presence of adhesion (modelled as a rate-independent Lennard Jones force-separation law), assuming for simplicity a single relaxation time of the material. In this model, there are no shear stresses at the interface, but only pressures, and these produce friction only because of the actual slope of the surface. We have shown that the problem and hence the friction coefficient depends on four dimensionless quantities: the Maugis–Tabor parameter 𝜆 , the dimensionless normal load 𝑊 , the dimensionless sliding velocity 𝑉 0,the ratio 𝑘 = 𝐸 0∕𝐸 ∞ of the linear viscoelastic material, so we have been able to span the most interesting range of all four of them. The results show that for small 𝜆 , friction is very small, even smaller than the Hunter solution as an effect of the deviation from Hertz solution due to the Lennard-Jones law when dealing with very small cylinders. The interesting case of large 𝜆 shows a more complex role of adhesion which gives non-monotonic dependence on both normal load and speed. The results provided are in qualitative agreement with the experiments of Grosch (1963) on smooth glass surfaces. Nevertheless, actual compar-ison with experiments is made difficult by the fact that on smooth surfaces, other mechanisms may contribute to rubber friction, which we have discussed. The case of large Maugis–Tabor parameter for example corresponds to large cylinders where it is likely that Schallamach waves are present or that friction is due to the Schallamach mechanism of bonding–debonding of rubber chains, rather than viscoelastic losses or the difference in adhesion at trailing and leading edge of the contact, as in the present model.
